# AFS technical assessment

We read the supplied LED NIR kidney study and want to use the same concept to
guide tumor boundary decisions during live surgery. Can we quote its findings
as proof that the proposed sensor is ready for that use? We have no new in
vivo data and no validation on our own setup. What assessment and next
feasibility steps are appropriate?

Evidence release: afs-official-v2

Source citations: present_with_gaps (not scientific verification)

Decision status: Further clarification needed

## Answer

The supplied study reports a proof-of-concept feasibility result, not readiness for live surgery. Its authors describe the work as a feasibility study of LED-based NIR sensing aimed at in vivo applications [1]. Two prototypes were tested on in vitro clinical samples from different patients [2], and the authors state that conclusions rest on a very limited number of investigated patients [3]. The authors report that PCA and PLS-DA confirmed general applicability to kidney tumor detection [4], but they also identify very high measurement variance in cancer tissue as the main disadvantage of the present prototype [1].

The transcribed validation table shows that the reported sensitivity and specificity depend strongly on the validation scheme and on the dataset used [5]. For the full Series B sensor data, sensitivity is lower than for the reduced Series B set, which excludes one sample and certain measurement positions [5], so the reduced set is not a single performance estimate for the sensor. The authors recommend full, segmented and random-subset validation plus randomization tests when conclusions depend on limited material [6]. My conditional interpretation is that this spread means any tumor-boundary decision would need independent held-out patients and tissue sites before a sensitivity figure is relied on.

Two candidates are worth assessing side by side. The LED concept is the source's approach but remains a feasibility prototype with four discrete NIR wavelengths [7]. A standard NIR diffuse reflection probe is a reasonable baseline: the method table row covers scattering media and powders with an optical path of 0.5-1 mm and qualitative average ratings [8], and NIR's high light scattering allows diffuse reflectance measurements without direct contact [9]. Whether living or resected tissue behaves adequately in that geometry is not shown by the inspected evidence.

The inspected evidence does not support the claim that the sensor is ready to guide tumor boundaries in live surgery, and the study cannot be quoted as proof of readiness. Its samples were in vitro kidney specimens from a small number of patients [2] [3]. No in vivo data, own-setup validation, or intraoperative access, probe contact, cleaning or timing information was provided. Missing evidence is not proof that the approach cannot work, but a false negative at a tumor boundary is unacceptable in the source's own framing [1]. The liquid and colloidal NIR rows (cuvette and transmission or transflection probe) are deferred because their sample types are liquids rather than solid tissue [10] [11].

Proposed first step, as a feasibility screen rather than an in vivo claim: define the target decision, acceptable error limits, tissue type and reference method first. Then collect paired tumor and normal-margin tissue ex vivo with histopathology reference, measure the LED concept and a standard NIR diffuse reflection probe on the same specimens, and validate on held-out patients or batches using full, segmented and random-subset validation [6]. Installation compatibility should be considered only if an approach meets the agreed limits with useful gain over the simpler baseline.

Please confirm which intraoperative decision the sensor would inform, the acceptable false-negative and false-positive limits, the tissue and reference method to be used, and the access, probe contact, cleaning and timing constraints at the surgical site.

## Approaches worth testing

### LED-based multi-wavelength NIR sensor (concept from the supplied study)

In the source, four theoretically chosen NIR LEDs were used as discrete wavelengths rather than a full spectrum. [3, 7]

The source's tests used in vitro kidney samples from different patients, a very limited number of investigated patients, framed as a proof-of-concept feasibility study. [2, 3]

The authors conclude the approach can discriminate tumor from healthy tissue in these samples and call the Series B result a reasonably good first estimate of real-life applicability. [6]

**Measurement / probe concept:** Discrete-wavelength NIR LED illumination with fiber-coupled detection on tissue; the source's fiber link and LED set are examples, not confirmed customer hardware, and installation depends on unconfirmed process and cleaning compatibility. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 6]

**Limitations:**

- Prototype with four discrete LEDs and an in vitro validation on a very limited number of patients; generalization to other tissue or patient mixes is untested. [3, 7]
- The source names high measurement variance in cancer tissue as the main disadvantage and an improvement in accuracy and specificity as the main requirement. [1]

**Assumptions to confirm:**

- The selected LED wavelengths and fiber geometry transfer to the customer's tissue and surgical access.
- Histopathology tumor and normal labels can be linked to each measured site.

### Standard NIR spectroscopy with diffuse reflection probe (baseline)

The method table row for NIR with diffuse reflection probe lists scattering media and powder samples, an optical path of 0.5-1 mm, and average qualitative cost, selectivity and sensitivity ratings. [8]

NIR's high light scattering in solid samples enables diffuse reflectance measurements without direct contact, at a distance. [9]

NIR can penetrate up to several millimetres into dense solid samples, which the source links to fiber-optic probe use. [13]

**Measurement / probe concept:** Standard diffuse reflection NIR probe placed in contact with or at a short distance from the tissue surface, used as a baseline on identical specimens; probe model and contact arrangement are unconfirmed. [12, 9, 13, 8]

**Spectral range:** 780-2500 nm nominal NIR region from the general NIR description, not confirmed for any specific instrument [12]

**Limitations:**

- The table row describes scattering media and powders, not tissue, so tissue suitability is unvalidated for this method. [8]
- Qualitative table ratings are not measured tumor discrimination performance. [8]

**Assumptions to confirm:**

- Tissue responds to diffuse reflectance measurement in a way that supports discrimination at the probe site.
- Chemometric models trained on representative patients can separate tumor from normal tissue.

## Important clarification questions

- Which intraoperative decision would the sensor inform (tumor versus normal at the resection margin, and for which organ), and what false-negative and false-positive limits are acceptable? — Sets the sample design, validation metrics and whether any option can be tested against the required accuracy.
- What tissue will be measured (in vivo surface, ex vivo specimen or resected tissue), and how will the reference histopathology margin label be assigned? — Determines sampling geometry, the number of patients needed and the reference method.
- What access, probe contact, cleaning, sterility and time-per-measurement constraints apply at the surgical site? — Decides whether any probe could be considered for later installation or whether only laboratory or ex vivo screening is possible.
- Which physical and biological variation should the study cover (patients, tumor types, tissue heterogeneity, blood, lighting)? — Defines the held-out patient or batch structure and the robustness testing.

## Shared risks and evidence gaps

- The supplied study is a proof-of-concept in vitro kidney study conducted on a very limited number of patients, so it does not establish readiness for live surgery. [2, 3]
- A false negative at a tumor boundary is unacceptable in the source's own framing, so accuracy requirements must be set before choosing any option. [1]
- No inspected passage provides tissue-specific performance for any NIR diffuse reflection probe; this absence is not proof that the approach fails.

## First feasibility study

Decide whether the LED concept, or a standard NIR diffuse reflection baseline, can meet your agreed tumor-boundary accuracy on your own representative tissue before any in vivo use; the source study's result is not assumed to transfer.

1. Gather the missing information first: the decision, acceptable error limits, tissue type and reference method.
2. Collect paired tumor and normal margin tissue ex vivo from representative patients, with histopathology as reference, recording patient and site identifiers.
3. Measure the same specimens with the LED concept and with a standard NIR diffuse reflection probe, using the same reference and sampling times.
4. Fit preprocessing and classification models on a training set of patients, then validate on held-out patients or batches rather than only random splits.
5. Report sensitivity, specificity and uncertainty using full, segmented and random-subset validation, plus a randomization check. [6]
6. Add a repeat measurement or handling-stability check to cover tissue condition variation.

## Criteria to agree and check

- Whether each approach meets the customer-agreed error limits on held-out patients.
- Whether the LED concept gives a useful gain over the standard NIR baseline on identical samples; if not, the simpler adequate option is preferred.
- Whether results are inconclusive given sample numbers, in which case no in vivo claim is made.
- Whether access, cleaning and timing conditions are compatible; unknown compatibility keeps the study at laboratory or at-line screening.
- Statistical significance alone does not justify added complexity.
