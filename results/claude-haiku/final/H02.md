# AFS technical assessment

Our dairy process handles both a relatively clear whey stream and an opaque
milk concentrate. We want a pilot for composition monitoring, using laboratory
protein and fat results where those variables are relevant. We can compare
different measurement interfaces before choosing the installation. What first
measurement concepts and comparison experiment would make sense for these two
streams?

Evidence release: afs-official-v2

Source citations: present_with_gaps (not scientific verification)

Decision status: Provisional feasibility assessment

## Answer

For the clear whey stream, the inspected material points to NIR transmission. It is described as the preferred NIR method for transparent or semi-transparent liquids such as skim milk, whey or permeate, with a transmission or transflection probe passing light through a defined sample layer [1]. The same material describes the optical pathlength as the key control, usually 1-10 mm, because a path that is too wide loses signal to absorption and one that is too narrow samples a negligible volume [1]. A passage on whey filtration streams describes transmission as superior for high-precision analysis of dilute process streams [2]. Conditional interpretation: this fit depends on the whey staying clear enough at a workable pathlength, which is not confirmed for your stream.

For the opaque milk concentrate, the inspected material points to NIR reflection. It is described as primarily used for opaque or highly concentrated dairy products, including whole milk concentrates, with one fiber set delivering light and another collecting backscattered light [3]. Reflection is described as sensitive to surface characteristics and fat globule size, and fat and protein quantification may use absorbance conversion with MSC or SNV preprocessing before PLS regression [4]. Conditional interpretation: reflection is the plausible starting point for the concentrate, but its representativeness depends on how the concentrate is sampled at the probe face.

A transflection probe is described as possibly a combined solution for low-fat and high-fat milk products, with the same NIR spectrometer and light source used for reflection and transmission [5]. The table lists transmission or transflection probes as a NIR geometry for liquid colloidal samples with a 1-10 mm optical path [6]. This supports only a milk-product hypothesis; the inspected material does not establish transflection performance for whey or milk concentrate, so it should be tested rather than assumed.

Proposed first study: use the same NIR spectrometer and light source, and measure paired whey and concentrate samples from across the production range with transmission, reflection and transflection interfaces. Pair every spectrum with laboratory fat and protein results and sampling times. Fit preprocessing and PLS models on training batches only, then validate on independent held-out batches or days. Compare each interface against the agreed error limits for each stream, and check whether one probe covers both streams before accepting two interfaces.

Please define the fat and protein target ranges and laboratory reference methods for each stream, including whether fat is relevant in the whey, and state the acceptable error limits and reference uncertainty. These answers determine which interface can meet the requirement and what validation counts as adequate.

The inspected sources are general process descriptions. No inspected passage reports fat or protein accuracy for whey or milk concentrate, and the table ratings are qualitative rather than measured performance [6] [7]. Fluorescence, Raman and MIR ATR are mentioned as options for specialized monitoring but are not assessed here [8]. Installation, cleaning and access compatibility are unknown, so the study should start with laboratory or at-line screening.

## Full assessment

## Approaches worth testing

### NIR transmission for clear whey

NIR transmission is described as the preferred method for transparent or semi-transparent liquids such as skim milk, whey or permeate. [1]

Optical pathlength is the key control, usually 1-10 mm; too wide loses signal to absorption and too narrow samples a negligible volume. [1]

Transmission is described as superior for high-precision analysis of dilute process streams such as whey filtration. [2]

**Measurement / probe concept:** Transmission cell or transmission probe with a defined pathlength, sampling a clear liquid layer; installation is conditional on process compatibility. [1, 2, 6, 9]

**Spectral range:** Nominal NIR method range 780-2500 nm per the method description; not confirmed system coverage. [1, 2, 6, 9]

**Limitations:**

- The inspected passage on whey filtration does not report accuracy for this customer's whey.
- Transmission fails when milk becomes too thick or fat-rich, so it does not cover the concentrate. [2]

**Assumptions to confirm:**

- The whey remains clear enough at the chosen pathlength for transmitted light to reach the detector.
- Fat and protein are quantifiable from transmission spectra of this whey at the expected concentrations.

### NIR diffuse reflection for opaque milk concentrate

Inline NIR reflection is primarily used for opaque or highly concentrated dairy products, including whole milk concentrates. [3]

Reflection is sensitive to surface characteristics and fat globule size, and models may use MSC or SNV preprocessing before PLS to quantify fat and protein. [4]

**Measurement / probe concept:** Flush-mounted fiber reflection probe with one fiber set for illumination and another for backscattered light; installation is conditional on process compatibility. [3, 4, 5, 7, 9]

**Spectral range:** Nominal NIR method range 780-2500 nm per the method description; not confirmed system coverage. [3, 4, 5, 7, 9]

**Limitations:**

- Reflection can be less representative for clear liquids, so it is unsuitable for the whey stream. [5]
- Results depend on surface characteristics, so probe fouling or deposits may affect readings; this is not established by the inspected sources.

**Assumptions to confirm:**

- The probe face is representative of the bulk concentrate.
- Preprocessing and PLS models built on training batches transfer to held-out concentrate batches.

### NIR transflection probe as a single-interface hypothesis

A transflection probe is described as possibly a combined solution for low-fat and high-fat milk products. [5]

**Measurement / probe concept:** Transflection probe combining transmission and reflection geometries, if the process permits; for testing only until compatibility is known. [5, 6]

**Limitations:**

- The inspected evidence covers milk products only, not whey or milk concentrate specifically.
- Benefit over separate interfaces is a hypothesis that the pilot must test.

**Assumptions to confirm:**

- The milk-product finding transfers to clear whey and to the milk concentrate.
- A single probe geometry can give a usable path for both streams.

## Important clarification questions

- What are the fat and protein target ranges and laboratory reference methods for the whey and the milk concentrate, including whether fat matters in the whey? — Determines whether each stream needs fat and protein models and which interface can meet the requirement.
- What acceptable fat and protein error limits, reference method uncertainty and sample timing can you agree for the pilot? — Sets the pass or fail criteria for comparing transmission, reflection and transflection.
- What process variation should the pilot cover, such as temperature, solids, fat and protein fluctuation, bubbles and fouling? — Determines the sample set needed for representative training and held-out validation.
- What access, cleaning and installation constraints apply at the whey and concentrate sampling points? — Determines whether any interface can be installed later and whether a single probe is practical.

## First feasibility study

Decide, for each stream, whether NIR transmission, reflection or a transflection probe gives fat and protein estimates within the agreed limits against laboratory references, and whether one interface can serve both streams.

1. Collect the agreed information on target ranges, reference methods, error limits, sample timing and process variation before measurements begin.
2. Collect representative whey and milk concentrate samples across the production range, recording sampling time and the laboratory fat and protein reference for each.
3. Measure each sample with the same NIR spectrometer and light source using a transmission cell, a reflection probe and a transflection probe; test pathlength options within the range the method description gives.
4. Fit preprocessing and PLS models on training batches only, applying MSC or SNV to reflection data where appropriate.
5. Validate each model on independent held-out batches or days and compare error and bias against the agreed limits for each stream.
6. Compare a transflection probe against the separate interfaces on the same material before accepting two interfaces.

## Criteria to agree and check

- Each interface meets the agreed fat and protein error limits on held-out batches for its stream, or the result is inconclusive.
- Whether one probe covers both streams with acceptable error; a simpler single interface is preferred if it is adequate and offers no meaningful loss.
- Whether the gain from a second interface justifies added complexity; statistical significance alone is not enough.
- Robustness across the process variation covered by the pilot.
- An inconclusive or no-adequate-candidate outcome is a valid result and should be reported as such.
