# AFS technical assessment

We read the supplied LED NIR kidney study and want to use the same concept to
guide tumor boundary decisions during live surgery. Can we quote its findings
as proof that the proposed sensor is ready for that use? We have no new in
vivo data and no validation on our own setup. What assessment and next
feasibility steps are appropriate?

Evidence release: afs-official-v2

Source citations: present_with_gaps (not scientific verification)

Decision status: Further clarification needed

## Answer

The inspected kidney study cannot be quoted as proof that the proposed sensor is ready for intraoperative tumor boundary guidance. It is a feasibility study on in vitro samples: seven pairs of tumor and normal samples from different patients, measured at a few selected wavelengths [1]. Its authors state that conclusions about general suitability rest on a very limited number of investigated patients [2]. They name very high measurement variance in cancer tissue as the main disadvantage and state that false negatives are unacceptable in oncological diagnostics [3]. In the Table 1 sensor-measurement datasets, segmented cross-validation gave lower sensitivity than full-data calibration [4].

The statistical analysis supports only general applicability to kidney tumor detection, checked with PCA, PLS-DA and sample permutation [5]. In my conditional reading, this justifies a next feasibility step for kidney tissue, but it does not show that a boundary decision during live surgery would meet the required error limits, because the inspected passages report no in vivo or intraoperative measurements. Segmented cross-validation is the more demanding split, and its lower sensitivity suggests that performance on new patients may be weaker than the full-data fit indicates. [4]

Readiness cannot be claimed from this evidence. The authors describe the study as a feasibility study aimed at in vivo applications, with further development still needed [3]. There are no in vivo data, no validation on the customer's surgical setup, and the patient base is small [2]. Missing evidence does not show that the approach cannot work; it means the requested certainty is not supported.

In the AFS method table, the NIR diffuse reflection probe row is for scattering media and powder, with an optical path of 0.5-1 mm and average ratings for cost, selectivity and sensitivity [6]. These are qualitative ratings, not measured accuracy for tissue [6]. The NIR cuvette and transmission or transflection probe rows describe liquid and colloidal samples, so I defer them for solid surgical tissue [7] [8].

Before any installation planning, I propose a paired ex vivo feasibility study on representative tumor and adjacent normal tissue. It would compare the LED-based concept with a full-spectrum diffuse reflection NIR baseline on the same samples, with each measurement paired to a histopathology reference and sampling time. Fitting and preprocessing should use one patient set, with validation on held-out patients.

Which boundary decision and reference method define success, and what false-negative, false-positive and response limits can you agree before testing?

## Approaches worth testing

### LED-based multi-wavelength NIR fiber sensor (source concept)

The source is a feasibility study of an LED-based NIR sensor for kidney tumor detection, using in vitro tumor and normal samples measured at a few selected wavelengths. [1]

PCA and PLS-DA were reported to confirm general applicability to kidney tumor detection, with sample permutation as an additional validation. [5]

In the Table 1 sensor-measurement datasets, segmented cross-validation gave lower sensitivity than full-data calibration, and the Bs140 subset, which excludes one sample and some measurement positions, had higher segmented sensitivity than the full Bs170 dataset. [4]

The authors name very high measurement variance in cancer tissue as the main disadvantage of the present sensor. [3]

**Measurement / probe concept:** Fiber-coupled LED-based NIR sensing at a few selected wavelengths, with tissue probed through a fiber interface; the exact source geometry is not described in the inspected passages. [3, 1, 5, 4]

**Limitations:**

- Tissue information is restricted to a few selected wavelengths, and no inspected passage compares it with a full-spectrum baseline.
- Segmented validation of the sensor data shows reduced sensitivity and high variance, so the source does not show reliable boundary classification.

**Assumptions to confirm:**

- The LED wavelengths selected for kidney tissue transfer to the customer's target tissue and boundary decision.
- The source measurement geometry can be reproduced at the surgical site, which the inspected passages do not describe.

### Full-spectrum NIR diffuse reflection fiber probe with chemometrics (baseline)

NIR spectra cover 780-2500 nm and contain overtone and combination bands of molecular vibrations. [9]

Diffuse reflectance in the NIR region can be measured without direct contact at a distance, and overlapping bands suggest chemometrics for analysis. [10]

The AFS diffuse reflection probe row covers scattering media and powder, with an optical path of 0.5-1 mm; its ratings are qualitative, not measured performance. [6]

NIR penetration into dense solid samples can reach several millimetres, which the source links to online fiber probes. [11]

**Measurement / probe concept:** Diffuse reflection fiber probe in contact or non-contact geometry at the tissue surface, recording full NIR spectra for chemometric analysis; the geometry depends on surgical access. [10, 6]

**Spectral range:** Nominal NIR region 780-2500 nm, as described for NIR spectrometry; not confirmed system coverage. [9, 10, 11, 6]

**Limitations:**

- No inspected passage shows this method on tumor tissue, so its tissue performance is not established.
- Overlapping NIR bands require chemometric models that depend on representative training data and independent validation.

**Assumptions to confirm:**

- Diffuse reflection from the target tissue at the surgical site is stable enough for chemometric models to be built and validated.
- Instrument cost and footprint are acceptable, which cannot be judged because the customer's budget is not stated.

## Important clarification questions

- Which tissue types must be distinguished, what counts as a boundary error, and which reference method (for example histopathology) defines the true boundary? — Defines the sample set, reference protocol and whether the LED wavelength set or a full-spectrum baseline is relevant.
- What surgical access and contact conditions apply, including probe contact or distance, blood or moisture, sterilization and cleaning, and the time available per decision? — Determines whether a fiber probe geometry is feasible and which bench conditions a representative study must reproduce.
- What false-negative, false-positive and response-time limits would you accept, and what budget applies to each candidate? — Sets the decision criteria and whether a simpler diffuse reflection baseline is adequate or added complexity is justified.

## Shared risks and evidence gaps

- The evidence is kidney-specific, in vitro, and drawn from seven patients, with no inspected in vivo or intraoperative measurement; results cannot be transferred to live surgery without validation. [3, 2]
- The inspected passages do not describe the source's LED wavelength set, probe geometry or reference histopathology protocol, so applicability to the customer's interface is unknown.
- Qualitative table ratings and nominal ranges are not measured performance for either candidate on tumor tissue.

## First feasibility study

Decide whether the LED-based concept and a full-spectrum NIR diffuse reflection baseline can distinguish agreed tumor and normal boundary classes on representative tissue, against customer-agreed error and response limits, before any in vivo use is considered.

1. Gather information first: confirm the boundary definition, tissue types, reference histopathology method, surgical geometry, cleaning constraints and the limits the customer agrees to.
2. Collect paired tumor and adjacent normal tissue from a representative number of patients under the required ethics approvals, recording histopathology reference and sampling time for each.
3. Measure the same samples with the LED-based sensor and a full-spectrum diffuse reflection probe in a fixed bench geometry that mimics the surgical interface, with repeats.
4. Include realistic physical variation such as blood, moisture, temperature and probe pressure, as agreed with the customer.
5. Fit preprocessing and models on a training subset of patients, then validate on held-out patients or batches not used for fitting.
6. Compare sensitivity, specificity, error rates and response time of both candidates against the agreed limits, with per-patient variation reported.

## Criteria to agree and check

- At least one candidate meets the customer-agreed error and response limits on held-out patients; if neither does, the evidence does not support the concept for this decision.
- If the full-spectrum baseline meets the limits, the LED-based approach's added complexity is not justified; if only the LED-based approach meets them, its added value is established only within this study.
- No useful gain, or inconclusive results with overlapping error ranges, are valid outcomes.
- Statistical significance alone does not establish practical usefulness.
- Ex vivo results do not establish in vivo or intraoperative applicability; that requires a later conditional phase.
- Cleaning, sterilization and safety compatibility are confirmed before any device work.
